The geometry of equilibrium
Scientific note ↓

Fourteen vertices.
One unstable vertex.

Fourteen vertices suffice to build a convex solid of uniform density that can balance on just one of its tips. The earlier examples compared in the paper have 16 and 18 vertices.

A double spiral with no apex

The vertices lie along two arms that meet at V0. Raising and lowering the rim puts more material near the bottom of the footprint. This shifts the volume centroid just far enough to give each of the other thirteen vertices an escape direction.

How thin can it get?

In the 14-vertex example, multiply every height by the same factor t. V0 remains the only unstable vertex for every 0 < t ≤ 1. The projected centroid stays in place. Move the slider to flatten the solid.

The result and the data

The example has 14 vertices, 36 edges and 24 triangular faces. In the original coordinates the stable faces are {0, 5, 7} and {0, 6, 13}. The saddle equilibrium lies on edge {6, 7}. Compared with the 18- and 16-vertex examples in the paper, this construction lowers the upper bound to 14 vertices. The minimum remains open.

The 13-vertex candidate has two unstable vertices, V0 and V12. It is one of the verified outcomes of the follow-up search. Take any twelve vertices of the original 14-vertex example, keep them fixed and add a thirteenth point anywhere in space. Whenever all thirteen points are vertices of the resulting convex hull, at least two are unstable. This exclusion applies to that family.

This page includes the rational coordinates, faces, volume centroids and witnesses for both models. The signs of the displayed inequalities are computed with exact integer arithmetic. The graphics use numerical projections. “Exaggerate z” changes only the scale of the drawing. The witness arrows identify edges with a positive dot product. In the thickness map, the arrow from L to C shows the centroid shift.
